<h2 style="margin: 60px 0px 10px;">Research</h2>

My research studies how information is acquired, processed, and incorporated into asset prices. It is organized around two related strands. The first uses noisy rational expectations equilibrium (NREE) models to study endogenous information acquisition and how market access, data-processing technology, and trading constraints shape information allocation and price informativeness. The second studies cognitive frictions in perception and belief formation. Across both strands, a common theme is that the availability of information alone does not determine how informed investors or markets become: market institutions shape which information investors acquire and process, while cognitive frictions shape how they interpret the information they receive.

<h3 style="margin: 35px 0px 10px;">Job Market Paper</h3>

<p><strong>Market Access and the Production of Information</strong> <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_JMP_market_access.pdf">[PDF]</a> <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_JMP_market_access_online_appendix.pdf">[Online Appendix]</a><br>

<em>Funding:</em> Chazen Institute for Global Business, Bernstein Center for Leadership and Ethics.<br>
<em>Presentations:</em> Columbia (2020, 2021, 2022); China International Forum on Finance and Policy (2026, scheduled).<br>

<em>Abstract:</em> Market access changes which assets investors can hold and, in turn, reshapes how they allocate scarce data-processing capacity across assets---including assets they cannot hold. Investors with partial access engage in cross-asset learning only when the auxiliary asset is informative enough to be useful but not so informative that its information is already efficiently revealed through prices. As a result, market access can separate different dimensions of informational efficiency: making an asset investable can reduce its price informativeness, while in other cases prices become more informative even as average posterior precision falls. A China calibration shows that partial market access can generate large equilibrium effects by substantially reshaping information allocation and price informativeness despite a small foreign ownership share. Expanding foreign investability further has little impact, while relaxing the short-sale constraint substantially improves price informativeness.</p>

<h3 style="margin: 35px 0px 10px;">Working Papers</h3>

<p><strong>Reading Tea Leaves: Benefits and Costs of Learning about Future Demand Shocks</strong> <em>(under review at the Journal of Economic Theory)</em> <a href="https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7489552">[SSRN]</a><br>
<em>Presentations:</em> AFA Poster Session (2021) <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_future_demand_shocks_AFA_poster.pdf">[Poster]</a> <a href="/assets/documents/AFA.mp3">[Audio]</a>; Finance Theory Group Summer School (2019) <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_future_demand_shocks_FTG_summer_school_slides.pdf">[Slides]</a>; Columbia (2018, 2019).<br>
<em>Abstract:</em> In the noisy rational expectations equilibrium (NREE) literature, existing studies have investigated acquisition of information about current demand shocks and current and future dividend innovations. This article fills an important gap by characterizing the equilibrium effects of learning about future demand shocks. It shows that with access to additional information about future demand shocks, investors learn less about current dividend innovations and demand shocks and allocate greater precision to signals about future demand shocks through the front-running channel and a novel future uncertainty channel. This future uncertainty channel operates on two levels and breaks the fallacy of composition: at the individual level, acquisition of information about future demand shocks reduces the conditional variance of future excess payoffs; in the aggregate, market-wide dividend information acquisition declines, rendering dividends riskier and raising risk premiums. This article also demonstrates that greater heterogeneity in private information increases price volatility and that learning about future demand shocks renders prices less informative contemporaneously but more informative in later periods.</p>


<p><strong>From Persistent Disagreement to Agreement: Learning with Revisions of Perception Sets</strong> <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_cognitive_frictions.pdf">[PDF]</a><br>
<em>Presentations:</em> Columbia (2021).<br>
<em>Abstract:</em> We often view sensation and perception as one process and mistakenly take our internal representation of the physical world as the world itself. Cognitive biases could occur in the perception process without our noticing. This paper zooms in on the learning process and highlights a specific subprocess---the perception process. Agents take the same sensory information as inputs, direct them through different perceptual sets and thus form different perceptual information, which is then used to update posterior beliefs. Biased perceptual sets thus lead to persistent disagreement among agents. A revision of the perceptual set can generate a discrete revision of posterior beliefs, even when no new raw information arrives. Full revisions align beliefs with the common-perception Bayesian benchmark; partial revisions narrow disagreement without necessarily eliminating it. </p>
