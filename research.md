<h2 style="margin: 60px 0px 10px;">Research</h2>

My research studies how information is acquired, processed, and incorporated into asset prices. It is organized around two related strands. The first uses noisy rational expectations equilibrium (NREE) models to study endogenous information acquisition and how market access, data-processing technology, and trading constraints shape information allocation and price informativeness. The second studies cognitive frictions in perception and belief formation. Across both strands, a common theme is that the availability of information alone does not determine how informed investors or markets become: market institutions shape which information investors acquire and process, while cognitive frictions shape how they interpret the information they receive.

<h3 style="margin: 35px 0px 10px;">Job Market Paper</h3>

<p><strong>Market Access and the Production of Information</strong> <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_JMP_market_access_full.pdf">[PDF]</a><br>

<em>Funding:</em> Chazen Institute for Global Business, Bernstein Center for Leadership and Ethics.<br>
<em>Presentations:</em> Columbia (2020, 2021, 2022); China International Forum on Finance and Policy (2026, scheduled).<br>

<em>Abstract:</em> Investors often face restrictions on the assets they may trade. Beyond risk sharing and capital flows, these restrictions can change what investors learn and what prices reveal. In a noisy rational expectations equilibrium model, I study how market access affects information production by reshaping the allocation of scarce data-processing capacity and the aggregation of information through prices. Investors with partial access may acquire information about assets they cannot hold because it helps them value assets they can trade. This cross-asset learning is strongest at intermediate levels of informativeness. Making an asset investable can reduce price informativeness, or increase it while lowering average posterior precision. Calibrating the model to the Chinese stock market, I find large informational effects despite small foreign ownership. Expanding foreign investability has little impact, whereas relaxing the short-sale constraint substantially improves price informativeness.</p>

<h3 style="margin: 35px 0px 10px;">Working Papers</h3>

<p><strong>Reading Tea Leaves: Benefits and Costs of Learning about Future Demand Shocks</strong> <em>(under review at the Journal of Economic Theory; <a href="https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7489552" style="color:#000; font-style:normal; text-decoration:none; font-family:'Menlo', 'Consolas', monospace; font-size:0.78em; letter-spacing:-0.03em;">DOI: 10.2139/ssrn.7489552</a>)</em> <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_future_demand_shocks.pdf">[PDF]</a><br>
<em>Presentations:</em> AFA Poster Session (2021) <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_future_demand_shocks_AFA_poster.pdf">[Poster]</a> <a href="/assets/documents/AFA.mp3">[Audio]</a>; Finance Theory Group Summer School (2019) <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_future_demand_shocks_FTG_summer_school_slides.pdf">[Slides]</a>; Columbia (2018, 2019).<br>
<em>Abstract:</em> In the noisy rational expectations equilibrium (NREE) literature, existing studies have investigated acquisition of information about current demand shocks and current and future dividend innovations. This article fills an important gap by characterizing the equilibrium effects of learning about future demand shocks. It shows that with access to additional information about future demand shocks, investors learn less about current dividend innovations and demand shocks and allocate greater precision to signals about future demand shocks through the front-running channel and a novel <em>future uncertainty channel</em>. This future uncertainty channel operates on two levels and breaks the fallacy of composition: at the individual level, acquisition of information about future demand shocks reduces the conditional variance of future excess payoffs; in the aggregate, market-wide dividend information acquisition declines, rendering dividends riskier and raising risk premiums. This article also demonstrates that greater heterogeneity in private information increases price volatility and that learning about future demand shocks renders prices less informative contemporaneously but more informative in later periods.</p>


<p><strong>From Persistent Disagreement to Agreement: Learning with Revisions of Perceptual Sets</strong> <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_cognitive_frictions.pdf">[PDF]</a><br>
<em>Presentations:</em> Columbia (2021).<br>
<em>Abstract:</em> We often view sensation and perception as one process and mistakenly take our internal representation of the physical world as the world itself. Cognitive biases could occur in the perception process without our noticing. This paper zooms in on the learning process and highlights a specific subprocess—the perception process. Agents take the same sensory information as inputs, direct them through different <em>perceptual sets</em> and thus form different <em>perceptual information</em>, which is then used to update posterior beliefs. Biased perceptual sets thus lead to persistent disagreement among agents, while <em>set revision</em> corrects any perceptual bias and allows agreement to be reached. Depending on the intensity of <em>perceptual shocks</em>, agents' sensitivity to perceptual shocks and their initial <em>entrenchment level</em>s, there could be overreaction or underreaction in the price to sensory information in the markets. Perceptual revisions can induce rebalancing, while a volume response requires an equilibrium portfolio specification. </p>
