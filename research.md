<h2 style="margin: 60px 0px 10px;">Research</h2>

My research studies how information is acquired, processed, and incorporated into asset prices. Two related strands organize my research. The first uses noisy rational expectations equilibrium (NREE) models to study endogenous information acquisition and the interaction of market access, data-processing technology, and trading constraints. The second studies cognitive frictions in perception and belief formation. Across both strands, a common theme is that a seemingly better information environment---more data, better processing technology, or broader market access---need not make markets more informative or investors better informed.

<h3 style="margin: 35px 0px 10px;">Job Market Paper</h3>

<p><strong>Market Access and the Production of Information</strong> <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_JMP_market_access.pdf">[PDF]</a> <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_JMP_market_access_online_appendix.pdf">[Online Appendix]</a><br>

<em>Funding:</em> Chazen Institute for Global Business, Bernstein Center for Leadership and Ethics.<br>
<em>Presentations:</em> Columbia (2020, 2021, 2022); China International Forum on Finance and Policy (2026, scheduled).<br>

<em>Abstract:</em> Market access changes which assets investors can hold and, in turn, reshapes how they allocate scarce data-processing capacity across assets---including assets they cannot hold. Investors with partial access engage in cross-asset learning only when the auxiliary asset is informative enough to be useful but not so informative that its information is already efficiently revealed through prices. As a result, market access can separate different dimensions of informational efficiency: making an asset investable can reduce its price informativeness, while in other cases prices become more informative even as average posterior precision falls. A China calibration shows that partial market access can generate large equilibrium effects by substantially reshaping information allocation and price informativeness despite a small foreign ownership share. Expanding foreign investability further has little impact, while relaxing the short-sale constraint substantially improves price informativeness.</p>

<h3 style="margin: 35px 0px 10px;">Working Papers</h3>

<p><strong>Reading the Tea Leaves: Benefits and Costs of Learning about Future Demand Shocks</strong> <em>(under review at the Journal of Economic Theory)</em> <a href="https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7489552">[SSRN]</a><br>
<em>Presentations:</em> AFA Poster Session (2021) <a href="/assets/documents/Ran_LIU_future_demand_shocks_AFA_poster.pdf">[Poster]</a> <a href="/assets/documents/AFA.mp3">[Audio]</a>; Finance Theory Group Summer School (2019); Columbia (2018, 2019).<br>
<em>Abstract:</em> This paper characterizes the equilibrium effects of learning about future demand shocks in a noisy rational expectations equilibrium. A novel future uncertainty channel breaks the fallacy of composition: while investors gain individually from lower conditional variances of future excess payoffs, aggregate acquisition of dividend information declines, making dividends riskier and raising risk premia. Heterogeneity in private information amplifies volatility, and learning about future demand shocks makes prices less informative today but more informative in the future.</p>


<p><strong>From Persistent Disagreement to Agreement: Learning with Revisions of Perception Sets</strong><br>
<em>Presentations:</em> Columbia (2021).<br>
<em>Abstract:</em> This paper examines perception as a distinct stage of learning. Agents process identical sensory information through heterogeneous perceptual sets, generating different perceptual signals and posterior beliefs. Biased perceptual sets give rise to persistent disagreement, while set revision corrects perceptual biases and leads to agreement. Depending on the intensity of perceptual shocks, agents’ sensitivities, and their initial levels of entrenchment, the market overreacts or underreacts to sensory information; trading volume rises when overreaction occurs. </p>
